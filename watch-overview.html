How to read this table — sources, columns, and the Value score

Specs and prices from each brand's site — treat as approximate.

L2L = lug-to-lug · Höhe = case height · "—" = not published by maker.

Leave Min/Max blank for no limit. Price sorts on an approx. EUR-equivalent (converted for $ listings).

Movement cost = approx. price to buy that caliber loose (parts market), not the brand's wholesale cost. "Not sold separately" = maker only supplies it inside a finished watch. Shown for reference only — the Value score rates the movement by architecture tier, not by parts price.

Value = how many spec points a watch sits above or below what this list charges for specs at its price. A spec score (0–100) is built from seven weighted criterion groups — movement, timekeeping, case & glass, water & lume, wearability, fittings and complications — then compared against a robust baseline of spec vs ln(price) fitted separately for mechanical and quartz. 0 = priced exactly as its specs predict; the badge shows the surplus or shortfall in spec points. Colour is banded rather than continuous: the residual carries about ±{{sigma}} spec points of noise, so watches within one band are ties, not a ranking. n/s = fewer than {{supportMin}} other watches within ±{{supportPct}}% of that price, so the list cannot say what it charges there. The Score column to its left is the same number on the 0–100 scale used up to revision 2 (50 = as predicted), kept for continuity; it sorts identically. Specs is step 1 alone — the weighted spec score with price taken out, answering “how much watch is this” rather than “how well is it priced”. It is the only one of the three defined for every row, but it correlates 0.85 with price and tilts toward tool watches, so read it alongside the other two rather than instead of them. Hover a badge for that watch’s inputs.

Value model, revision 3 — five changes, all to how the score is computed rather than to the underlying research. (1) The eleven criteria are now seven groups sharing one weight each. Measured on these rows the eleven were not independent — water and lume correlated at 0.75, movement tier and finishing at 0.72, tier and antimagnetism at 0.66, and the set carried roughly 6.4 effective dimensions — so correlated criteria stacked and “is it a diver” effectively commanded a fifth of the weight. (2) Wearability enters the score at 11%, taken from that freed weight: thickness and lug-to-lug as ratios of diameter, both already in the data and neither previously used. Divers lose ground here, deliberately — depth rating is bought with thickness and revision 2 counted the benefit without the cost. (3) The display back has its own weight with proper redistribution. Averaging it with the clasp meant confirmed-solid rows scored 0.293 against 0.432 for unresearched ones, so every research pass lowered the rows it touched; the Sinn 556 out-scored the Certina steel on fittings while having the worse clasp. (4) The baseline is Theil–Sen rather than least squares, with a separate intercept per movement class. OLS let single rows set the yardstick — the €51 Tithi carried a leverage of 0.186 and topped the whole list — and one line across two populations put mechanical watches 3.2 spec points below quartz at equal price, for a mean Value of 47.6 against 53.7. (5) Rows with fewer than five comparable prices are no longer scored, because the old curve extrapolated to an expected spec of 15.7 at €51 and crossed zero at €15. The badge now reads in signed spec points with a banded colour instead of a 0–100 integer on a continuous ramp, which implied a resolution the inputs never had. Depreciation, service cost and warranty stay out of the score by request; street price is wired in (streetPriceEUR) but no row carries one yet, and it remains the largest known bias, since list price is the right number for a micro-brand and the wrong one for a Swiss group brand that street-discounts 20–30%.

Model validation: computing…

Weight and German street prices, pass 10. Weight is now a column, and it is an estimate rather than research, because the research does not exist: searching all 67 rows produced exactly one measured figure (148 g for the Tissot Seastar 1000 40 mm Powermatic, from a review). Makers do not publish mass. Rather than invent 66 numbers, the column computes them from case diameter, thickness, material density and band type, with the method written out in the code so it can be checked or replaced. Against the single measured watch the estimator lands within 5%. It is not scored, and not because of caution: it is a deterministic function of diameter, thickness, case material and band, all of which the spec score already uses, so scoring it would double-count — titanium already scores above steel on the case criterion largely because it is light. German street prices were checked and the finding contradicts what this file previously assumed. The expectation was a 20–30% gap between list and street for the Swiss group brands, large enough to distort every comparison against the direct-selling micro-brands. It is not there. Across the German price comparison sites the Tissot Seastar references sit at or within a few percent of the prices already recorded here — the 40 mm Powermatic at €795 and the 38 mm chronograph at €575 are identical, the 36 mm quartz €20 lower — and the Certina titanium is the widest gap found at about 8%, with several shops still asking full price. Three rows carry a streetPriceEUR as a result and the rest are left alone, since a difference smaller than the measurement noise is not worth encoding. The residual sigma is about 4 spec points; an 8% price cut moves a row 1.3 points. So the largest known bias in this model turns out to be much smaller than advertised, and the earlier warning about it overstated the case. Two caveats remain: prices were sampled once and move constantly, and the comparison sites cover Swiss and Japanese brands well but carry almost nothing for Vaer, Lorier, AV86, MAEN, Earthen, Revelot, Delhi Watch Co. or Heinrich, which sell direct at a fixed price and therefore have no street price to find. That asymmetry is itself the answer: there is no discount to correct for on those rows.

Weight, pass 11 — the column is now mostly researched, and pass 10's central claim was wrong. Pass 10 concluded that mass "is simply not in the spec sheets", found one measured figure across all 67 rows, and estimated the other 66. Opening all 67 maker pages one at a time contradicts that flatly: four makers publish weight as an ordinary field, right under the case dimensions. Tissot give a "Gewicht (g)" on all nine of their pages, Citizen a "Gewicht" on twelve of fourteen, Seagull a "Net Weight" on all three, and Seiko one as well — 25 rows outright. Three more are sourced where the maker omits it: the Fujitsubo NB6025-59H at 111 g from Long Island Watch, the MoonSwatch at 29 g from a review spec table, and the HydroConquest at 167 g from a review that separates the three-link bracelet from the 155 g mesh. So 28 of 67 rows are now published figures rather than estimates, shown in bold. The failure in pass 10 was one of method rather than diligence — it searched for the number instead of reading the spec tables, and a search does not surface a field that is only rendered inside a product page. The 28 figures also make the estimator testable, and this is the rare case where the test set is genuinely clean: the estimator was written before any of these were known and none were used to tune it. It lands within ±15% on 25 of 28, with a median error of 6.1%, an RMSE of 11.8 g, and a mean signed error of +2.6%. Pass 10's ±15% was therefore roughly honest, if optimistic at the tails. Two biases are visible and are left uncorrected on purpose: titanium reads about 6% light across five rows, and the single bioceramic row that could be checked came out 45% heavy — 42 g estimated against 29 g published — which makes the two remaining Swatch estimates the weakest numbers in the column. Fixing either would mean fitting the estimator to the rows that are meant to test it. Two things were found wrong in the existing data. Row 21, the black PVD Seastar chronograph, was listed on a steel bracelet; Tissot's own page for that reference says "Silikon", and the 79 g they publish is a strap figure against 131 g for the steel-bracelet versions of the identical watch — corrected. And row 43's 183 g could not be reproduced from any source: Citizen's German and US pages both omit it, the row's link now lands on a category listing because the limited edition appears to have been delisted, and a US retailer reportedly shows 157 g. Two unverifiable numbers 26 g apart is not a measurement, so it was removed rather than kept, and the row falls back to the estimate. Vaer is a case worth naming. Its pages do carry "case weight of 70.6 grams" — but the identical sentence appears on the 36 mm and the 39 mm DS2, and on the 11.9 mm D4 and D5 as well as the 10.4 mm DS2. One figure cannot describe four different cases, so it is marketing boilerplate rather than a measurement and none of those seven rows use it. Seven rows remain unresolved, and that is a different statement from unpublished: the three Certina rows, the Swatch Royal Pop, the Lorier, the NOMOS and the Citizen Series 8 831 all failed on the fetch rather than on the spec sheet — certina.com and swatch.com timed out on every attempt, NOMOS returns 403, and Lorier's product URL 404s. Certina in particular appears to carry a net-weight field, so those are probably recoverable on a better connection. Everything else in the column — 31 rows across MAEN, Baltic, Orient, AV86, Earthen, Delhi, Vaer, Dan Henry, Heinrich, Revelot, two Citizens and the Blancpain Swatch — was checked page by page and genuinely does not publish a weight. One further figure exists but is not used: Sinn state "Gewicht ohne Band: 65 Gramm" for the 556, explicitly without the strap, and grafting an unmeasured bracelet onto a measured head would produce a number that is neither published nor estimated.

Watch type, pass 9. Every row now carries a type — diver, GMT/traveller, chronograph, pilot, sports, dress, field, everyday or pocket — exposed as a filter above the table and deliberately not as a column. It is data only and forms no part of any score. Type describes what a watch is for, which is a judgement rather than a specification, and scoring it would put a preference inside a column meant to report facts; it also correlates hard with things already scored, so weighting it would double-count depth rating, bezel and lume. Watches carry every type that applies and the filter matches on any of them, so the three Seastar chronographs appear under both Diver and Chronograph and the two Vaer G5s under both GMT and Diver. Forcing one label each would misfile the hybrids, which is much of what is interesting here. Where a maker states the intent the maker wins — Sinn call the 556 a pilot-style instrument watch, NOMOS call the Club a sports watch — otherwise it is read off depth rating, bezel, case and dial layout. Two judgement calls worth naming: Citizen’s Tsuyosa “Shore” has a rotating bezel but only 100 m and no ISO rating, so it is filed as Sports rather than Diver; and the three Vaer G2s sit under GMT alone, since their bezel is a GMT scale rather than a dive scale, while the G5s at 200 m earn both. The distribution is lopsided — 24 divers against one pilot and one pocket watch — so the counts are shown in the dropdown rather than left to be discovered after filtering.

Seven of the eight Mido rows land inside ±11%; the exception is the Ocean Star 36.5, estimated 16% heavy, which is the small-case end of the same pattern the Seiko Presage and the 40 mm Seagull already show. Three fields Mido does not publish for any reference, and they are the interesting ones. Lug-to-lug: taken from retailer and review spec tables for seven rows, and left empty on the Baroncelli, where the only figure found anywhere — 37 mm on a 38 mm case — is impossible on a lugged watch and was discarded rather than used. Bezel insert material: also third-party, and it matters, because the Ocean Star 36.5 turns out to use a domed mineral-glass bezel ring where the rest of the line uses aluminium — scored between the two at 0.6, the only entry in that column with that value. Movement tiers. The Caliber 80 is the Powermatic 80 — same C07 base, same synthetic escapement — so it takes the same 0.55, dropping to 0.50 for the Baroncelli, the one reference where Nivachron is claimed nowhere. Two sit above: the C07.661 in the GMT, at 0.60 for a true traveller GMT with a jumping local hour, the same +0.05 this list already pays for the Miyota 9075 over the 9015; and the Caliber 72, also 0.60, because the A31.111 is not a C07 at all but the 2892 lineage, with a conventional escapement and a real regulator rather than moulded parts and one-shot laser adjustment. What the model makes of them. Nothing dramatic, which is itself informative: six of the eight land within ±3 spec points of the baseline and none is more than 7 away, so the eight span 9.6 points end to end against 15.6 for the nine Tissots. The Multifort 8 One Crown is the outlier at +6.7 — 9.9 mm thick, sapphire display back, integrated bracelet and a Caliber 80 for €990 — and the Ocean Star 200 follows at +3.1. The two Special Editions pay for their second strap: the Ocean Star 39 at €1,320 sits 1.3 points below the mechanically identical two-tone at €1,190. One consequence to be aware of. Because these rows cluster so tightly, the residual sigma the colour bands are quantised on fell from 5.5 to 4.4 spec points, which tightened every band. Nine existing rows changed colour on that alone — their own residuals moved by 0.6 points at most, median 0.2. This is step 2 behaving as designed rather than a data change, and the model got better on its own test: leave-one-out RMSE 6.3 → 5.9 spec points, skill 59% → 64%, worst-case resampling shift 1.5 → 1.0. Still open. Mido's band field for the Ocean Star GMT reads "Textil / Dornschließe", but the product photography, every retailer listing and the 167 g Mido itself publishes all describe the steel bracelet, so the row is recorded on the bracelet and the discrepancy noted on it. Accuracy, pass 13 — the three ETA A31 rows (67 Longines HydroConquest, 70 and 71 Mido Ocean Star 39) chased again, on the reasoning that they share a movement family and might be resolvable together. All three keep an empty Accuracy column, and the search is now closed rather than merely unsuccessful. The A31 is the ETA 2892-A2 lineage, sold only inside Swatch Group, and neither the watch brand nor ETA publishes a rate for the specific variants used here. What circulates instead are two numbers that look like specifications and are not. −5/+15 s/day is the generic non-COSC A31 factory tolerance, not a figure either maker states for the L888.5 or the A31.111 — pass 6 already rejected it for the Longines and it fails the same test for the Mido. −4/+6 s/day is the COSC standard, and it attaches to the L888.4 and L888.6, not the L888.5 in this row; it leaks into the L888.5's spec sheets because at least one widely-cited caliber guide has the .4 and .5 the wrong way round, listing the L888.5 as the certified one. Caliber Corner's variant table is explicit the other way — .4 silicon plus COSC, .5 silicon without it — which is what this file has said since pass 6. Borrowing an ETA grade tolerance was the one route not tried before, and it fails too: Mido states the Caliber 72 as adjusted in four positions, and ETA's published grades are two positions (Standard), three (Elaboré) and five (Top), so there is no grade whose tolerance the row could inherit. That leaves the Powermatic precedent — Certina borrowing Swatch Group's published tier for the same base — unavailable here, because for the A31 there is no published tier. The one thing that did come back is measured data, and only for the Longines. Row 67 now carries a Measured figure of ≈1–3 s/day at fair confidence: a timegrapher reading of 0 s/day with under 3 s across six positions, a second owner at 1–2 s/day over several days, and a third who canvassed roughly twenty other owners and reports every L888 inside about 2 s/day — against outliers at +10, +12 and −5, which is why it is fair and not good. This is the first row in the table where the Measured column says something and the published column cannot, and it is worth naming what that implies: the HydroConquest is almost certainly the most accurate mechanical row here after the NOMOS, and the model has no way to know, because Value runs on the published column and row 67's is imputed at the list average. The Mido Caliber 72 gets nothing in either column — the only report found is a reviewer noting a loaner showed "no noticeable gains or lags", which is an impression rather than a rate — so rows 70 and 71 stay blank in both, and the A31.111's Mido exclusivity means no sibling caliber's data can stand in for it either.

Movement and accuracy audit, pass 14 — all 75 rows checked caliber by caliber against the maker's own page or the movement's datasheet, the first pass to go after the Movement and Accuracy columns as a pair rather than one at a time. Eight figures were wrong and are corrected. Vaer publish a timing tolerance on every product page and four rows did not match it: the three Miyota rows (D5 Meridian, both G5 Pacific) carried −5/+15 s/day where Vaer state −5/+10, and the D4 solar carried ±20 s/mo where Vaer give it the same −10/+20 s/mo as their quartz rows. That is one error running in both directions — the automatics under-credited, the solar over-penalised — and it survived earlier passes because the twelve Vaer rows had been checked for fittings, casebacks and weight but never for rate. Citizen contradict themselves between references on a single caliber, which is the more interesting find. Three Tsuyosa rows share cal. 8210, and Citizen's own pages give NJ0230 and NJ0231 as −20/+40 s/day but NJ0151-88M as ±20. Row 52 now follows its own reference rather than the caliber, on the principle this column has always used: the maker's statement about the watch in front of it wins over the family figure. Two power reserves were wrong alongside — the Zenshin Eco-Drive's J800 is 240 days, not the 180 that every other Eco-Drive here runs, and the "Shore" NJ0231 is a flat 42 h rather than the hedged ~40–42. And one caliber does not exist. Row 17, the PR516, was listed as Powermatic 80.711; Tissot's documented C07 variants are 80.111, 80.121, 80.601, 80.661 and 80.811, and the row's own parenthetical already said C07.111, which is the 80.111. That mattered beyond the label, because MOVEMENT_TIER keys on the exact movement string: the typo bought the PR516 a tier of 0.55 while the mechanically identical Seastar 1000 Powermatic sat at 0.50 on a separate entry — a split that would only make sense if one had Nivachron and the other did not, and Tissot advertise the Nivachron spiral on both references' own pages. The two are now one entry at 0.55, and row 24's residual rises about half a point. Row 21 carried a similar artefact: its "ETA G10.212/G15" was a third tier key for a movement rows 20 and 22 both list as plain G10.212, and G15 is an unrelated ETA chronograph family.

What pass 14 did not change is worth as much as what it did. The claim above that every accuracy figure other than the NOMOS is the maker's own spec was overstated for eight rows, and they are named here rather than quietly rewritten. Seagull publish caliber, jewels, frequency and power reserve for all three of their watches and no rate at all, so the −10/+20 s/day on rows 57–59 is the ST21 family figure rather than theirs. Neither AV86 nor Seagull publish anything for the ST17, so the ±30 s/day on rows 8 and 11 is an estimate. Sinn name the SW 200-1 without a grade or a rate, so row 60's ~±7 is read off Sellita's Élaboré tier — which does specify ±7 rising to ±20, so the inference is sound, it is just an inference. MAEN publish neither, so row 1's ±12 is La Joux-Perret's lowest G100 grade. And row 25 assumes the Seastar quartz GMT's F06.865 is not PreciDrive, unlike its confirmed-HAQ siblings at ±10 s/yr; owner reports say it is not, Tissot say nothing either way. All five inferences are left standing. The change is that the column now says which is which. One trap is recorded so a later pass does not walk into it. Citizen's German page for the Series 8 830 lists "+/- 15 Sek. pro Monat" among the caliber functions of a hand-wound-and-hacking mechanical — Eco-Drive boilerplate pasted onto the wrong movement — and row 42's −5/+10 s/day is Citizen's real published figure for cal. 0950 and stays. Row 53 (NJ0221-50E) is the one row left genuinely unresolved: Citizen's page returned ±20 s/day in one field and −20/+40 in another, so it keeps −20/+40 and the ambiguity is recorded rather than guessed away. Everything else checks out — Miyota 9039 and 90S5 at −10/+30, Orient F6722, the NH35, the VH31, 6P20 and 6P24, the ETA 7001 Top Grade, Certina's Powermatic 80.611, the PreciDrive F05.412 and F06.412 at ±10 s/yr, Seiko 6R51, Citizen 9051, 8322, 8213, E168, E111, J800 and 1502, SISTEM51 and the hand-wound SISTEM51-M, the MoonSwatch's G10.212 AB ND, Mido's Caliber 72 as the ETA A31.111, and the deliberately empty rate on the L888.5 and the two Caliber 72 rows. Effect on the model, which is small and in the right direction. Five rows gain a spec point each and row 24 about half of one; leave-one-out RMSE falls 5.94 → 5.93 and skill rises 63.9% → 64.1%. The residual sigma is unchanged at 4.3, so unlike pass 12 the bands themselves did not move: 68 of the 75 shift by 0.2 residual points or less, and beyond the five rows whose data changed the only ones to move further are rows 24 and 42, at about 0.5 and 0.7. Only two rows change colour — row 42 from as-expected to above, and row 9 across the below boundary on 0.05 of a point. A correction that leaves the ranking essentially intact is the outcome to want here, since it means the five wrong figures were not load-bearing.

Timex, pass 15 — one row added (76), the Deepwater Arctic 40.5 mm on the bracelet at €289, and the list goes from 75 to 76. It is the first Timex here and the cheapest steel diver in the table, and it is worth adding because it attacks the specification list from an angle nothing else here does: sapphire with anti-reflective coating, a ceramic bezel insert, 200 m with a screw-down crown and screw-on back, Super-LumiNova and a butterfly-clasp five-link bracelet, at a price where this list otherwise buys a mineral-glass quartz. Timex also become the sixth maker to publish mass as an ordinary field, and the most precise of them — 135.66 g, to two decimals, against Mido's one on the Tribute — so the weight column goes to 37 published figures of 76 and the estimator's clean test set from 36 rows to 37. What they do not publish is the movement. A follow-up on the bronze case score, checked after the rows landed and resolved against the change. The Bronze category now covers two different alloys and its 0.45 was argued, in pass 17, on only one of them: row 89 is CuSn8, a tin bronze, while row 126 is the CuAl8 aluminium bronze Baltic name outright. One real hole is left rather than papered over: aluminium bronze forms an adherent alumina film and is specified for seawater service, so row 126 genuinely patinates more slowly and less freely than row 89, which makes "it oxidises on purpose" a weaker claim here than there. Rescaling to use the full range would roughly double every complication’s effect, which the market evidence says would overshoot.

Accuracy, pass 7 — two changes. The Powermatic 80 figures were wrong. All five rows (three Certina, two Tissot) carried −15/+15 s/day, which is the old blanket number. Tissot now publish three tiers on their own site: a standard version at ±7 regulated to +3, giving −4 to +10, seen in the Classic Dream; an Elaborated version at ±5 regulated to +3, giving −2 to +8, used in the Le Locle and PRX; and a COSC version at −4/+6 in the Ballade. All five rows now read −4/+10, the most conservative published tier. Two caveats worth keeping: Tissot do not name the Seastar in any tier, so it may in fact be the Elaborated grade and would then be understated here; and Certina publish no rate figure for the 80.611 at all, so their three rows borrow Swatch Group’s published spec for the same ETA base rather than a Certina number. Second, a Measured column sits beside the published one, holding what watches on each caliber are actually reported to do. It is not scored — the Value model still runs on the published figure — and it exists to make the choice between the two standards visible. The gap is not a constant: roughly 1.7× on the Miyota 9015, 2× on Citizen’s cal. 8210, 2.5× on the NH35 and Miyota 9039, 2.7× on the Sellita, 2.3–3.5× on the Powermatic 80 and 5× on the NOMOS. Rebasing on measured rates would not rescale the column, it would hand several spec points to whichever makers quote most conservatively. Worth noting that fixing the Powermatic figure narrowed its own gap sharply, from about 4× against the stale −15/+15 to 2.3–3.5× against Tissot’s current spec — some of what looks like maker conservatism is simply out-of-date data. Thirty of the seventy-five rows carry a measured figure. The rest are blank because nothing is published: no review or owner log gives rates for the Seagull dress calibers, the La Joux-Perret G100, the Sistem51, the ETA 7001 as fitted here, or the smaller Citizen automatics, and essentially no one measures quartz. Confidence is marked per caliber in the tooltip, and the weakest entry — Citizen’s cal. 8210, where one owner reports every Citizen automatic inside ±3–5 and another returned a new one at +21 — is shown greyed and italic. None of these are controlled measurements. They are owner reports and review samples, posted mostly by people either delighted or annoyed, often taken with phone apps rather than timegraphers, and a magnetised watch reads exactly like a badly regulated one.

Accuracy correction, NOMOS Club Campus (row 66). This row carried ±1 s/day, which gave it a perfect 1.00 on the accuracy criterion — the only perfect score in the table, and seven times better than the next-best mechanical here. It does not come from NOMOS. Their own FAQ, repeated across three pages of their site, states that NOMOS mechanical watches achieve an accuracy of less than ten seconds deviation per day, regulated in six positions over seven days. Elsewhere they claim their calibers match chronometer results in in-house testing, but that is a comparison rather than a published figure, and chronometer standard is −4/+6 s/day, not ±1. Nothing NOMOS publishes for the DUW 4001 supports ±1, so the row was first set to their FAQ figure of < 10 s/day, which cut the sub-score from 1.00 to 0.32 and the spec score from 66 to 59. It now reads −4/+6 s/day, and that choice needs stating plainly because it is the one row in this column not taken from a spec sheet. NOMOS claim their calibers reach the same accuracy in in-house testing as chronometer-tested watches; chronometer standard under ISO 3159 is −4/+6, so the figure is derived from a NOMOS statement rather than from owner reports — but it is a comparative claim, not a guarantee, and most brands here make some version of it. The row is an exception, and if the column is ever audited this is the entry to revisit first. Two things make it defensible anyway. It is worth 2 spec points, against a residual sigma of about 5, on a row that carries no Value score at all for want of comparable prices — so nothing downstream turns on it. And it removes a mechanical artefact: < 10 is a single ceiling while −10/+30 is a range, and the parser reads a ceiling at its worst case but a range near its middle, so NOMOS was being scored at its guarantee while Miyota was scored comfortably inside theirs. Against the Measured column the row now sits at 2.5× conservatism, in line with the 1.7–3.5× of everything else, where < 10 had it as a lone 5× outlier. The measured record, searched afterwards, says the ±1 was not invented: it is roughly what this watch does. Wristworthy timed a Club Campus 38 against an atomic clock over a full loan period and averaged 1.2 s/day, noting that this clears both COSC and Rolex's in-house standard while NOMOS claims neither. A Club Campus owner on WatchUSeek logged an average of +0.9 s/day; another owner's Club sits at +1; a Club Campus neomatik at +3; an Orion 33 on the same caliber was reviewed at +3 to +5. So the hand-wound cluster genuinely sits around 1–3 s/day, far inside the published bound. The outliers that do appear — a Ludwig at +15, an Alpha back from service at +10 — are attributed by owners to magnetisation rather than regulation, and NOMOS calibers are widely reported to magnetise easily; the +10 example returned to ±0 after demagnetising. The correction therefore is not that ±1 was wrong about the watch. It is that ±1 was a measured figure sitting in a column of manufacturer specifications, where it competed against −5/+15 quoted by makers who are deliberately conservative. Scoring one row on observed performance and thirty-nine on published tolerance rewards the brand somebody happened to look up. If this column is ever rebased on measured rates, that is a defensible standard — it just has to be applied to all forty mechanicals.
